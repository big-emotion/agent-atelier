# Kuleshov effect — the montage that argues

**Status:** borrowed
**Filed:** 2026-10-04
**Source:** brought in by the operator on 2026-10-04 as screenshots of three
pages about Canal+'s *Le Zapping* (1989–2016, created by Patrick Menais):

- French Wikipedia, « Le Zapping », section « Description et historique » —
  a tertiary encyclopedia describing the format. It also reports the line
  below, with a footnote.
- Bruno Donnet on France Inter, 2015, as quoted by that article: « toute
  l'intelligence du zapping repose sur ce qu'on appelle l'effet Koulechov ».
  A practitioner (a media critic) naming the mechanism, not a study.
- TV Magazine / Le Figaro, « Le Zapping de Canal+ : la petite histoire de ce
  programme », 20 October 2015 — press, carrying the worked example below.

The underlying idea is Lev Kuleshov's 1920s film-editing experiment: one
actor's neutral face, cut against different shots, read as hunger, grief or
desire. **The original footage is lost and the experiment is known mostly
through later accounts; modern replications are mixed.** Treat it as the name of
a montage principle, not as a measured finding, and do not cite it publicly
without checking it first-hand.

## What it is

Two images placed side by side make a meaning that neither one holds alone. The
viewer draws the conclusion, and since nobody stated it, the viewer does not
argue with it. *Le Zapping* used no voice-over at all: the opinion was carried
entirely by **which** clips were chosen and **in what order** — « la sélection
des diverses séquences est subjective, et le montage, la manière dont elles
s'enchaînent, permet de soutenir une comparaison entre elles, afin par exemple
d'en souligner l'absurdité » (Wikipedia FR).

Worked example (TV Mag, 2015): after Canal+'s owner pulled a documentary about
Crédit Mutuel, the show ran extracts of it with clips from the game show *Motus*
spelling out the word « copinage » [cronyism] cut in between. Not one word of
commentary, and every viewer got the point.

## How it applies to a vertical montage without narration

This is what carries a commentary-free compilation such as EthniAfrica's planned
« Le Swipe »: found clips on one theme, no narration, original audio only.

- **The message is the unit, the theme is only a tag.** A single theme can give
  several montages, because the argument lives in the order, not in the subject.
  Write the message down in one sentence before cutting anything. It is never
  shown on screen, but it is what the cut is judged against.
- **A loop needs a second clip to close it.** The tension comes from the gap
  between two clips (A says this, B shows that), so the Zeigarnik loop is opened
  by placing clips side by side, not by a sentence. The first cut has to open a
  gap the viewer wants closed. A first clip that fits too comfortably with the
  second opens nothing.
- **The reframe is a clip, not a line.** With no narrator to say it, beat 5 is
  the clip that makes the whole sequence read differently. Choose it first and
  build backwards from it.
- **The cut sits on a completed thought.** Cutting a speaker off mid-sentence
  reads as a gag (*Le Zapping*'s satirical register). For a sourced atlas, cut
  on a full sentence and hold just long enough for the subtitle to be read.

## The fork

The power to argue without saying anything is also the power to make a speaker
say what they never meant. Kuleshov's experiment shows exactly that: the face did
not change, only what surrounded it. *Le Zapping* used this openly, for satire,
and its audience knew it was watching an editorial voice. A sourced atlas has no
such cover. Its reader assumes the clips are evidence.

Our rules:

1. **Never put a clip where it implies the opposite of what its speaker meant
   in the original.** Placing a clip next to another may add a meaning. It may
   not reverse the clip's own meaning. Test: could the speaker watch the cut and
   recognise their own point?
2. **What the sequence implies must rest on a source**, exactly as a sentence
   would. If the project could not write the message as a sourced claim, it
   cannot imply it either. Implication does not let a claim skip the source
   requirement.
3. **Every clip is credited on screen, with title, author and year, while it
   plays.** The viewer must be able to tell an archive from today's footage,
   and a speaker in 1962 from one in 2024. Dropping the date is how putting two
   clips side by side turns into falsifying one.
4. **No mockery of the people shown.** *Le Zapping* stressed absurdity. When the
   subject is how a people was named, a montage that laughs at a speaker from
   that people is contempt, and contempt does not get shared. Point the
   absurdity at the act of naming and at whoever imposed the name, never at
   whoever bears it.
5. **The message sentence is written down even though it is never shown.**
   That sentence is what the editorial review checks. A montage whose message
   nobody wrote cannot be checked, and a message nobody can check is the
   rhetoric that grows into an apparently sourced claim.
